Group Study Guide

Romans, together — in four sessions.

Romans is Paul's longest, most carefully argued letter — which makes it one of the best books to study as a group, and one of the easiest to get lost in without a map. This guide follows the letter's own structure (laid out in Formation's inductive study guide to Romans) across four sessions, each with an icebreaker, core discussion questions, a leader note on the passage's trickiest point, and a closing prompt.

groups 4 Sessions · Romans 1–16 · BSB Translation

How to use this guide

Ask everyone to read the session's full passage range before you meet — the questions below assume the group has already read it, not that you'll read it together for the first time live. As leader, you don't need to have every answer prepared; the leader notes exist to flag the one or two places a passage commonly gets misread or debated, not to give you a script. Plan on 45–60 minutes per session, and don't feel obligated to use every question — better to go deep on two than skim all four.

Session 1

Why We All Need Rescuing

Romans 1–3

Paul spends three chapters proving something uncomfortable before he ever offers the solution: that no one — not the openly irreligious, not the morally respectable, not even God's own covenant people — has any grounds to claim they don't need rescuing. This session works best if the group sits with the diagnosis before rushing ahead to the cure, which doesn't arrive until 3:21.

Warm-up: Before you'd ever read a chapter of Romans, what did you assume it was mainly about?

"for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God," — Romans 3:23, BSB

Discussion:

  1. Read Romans 1:18-32. Paul starts with the most obviously irreligious behavior. Why might that be an easy place for a group to agree — and a harder place to actually feel implicated?
  2. Read Romans 2:1-3. Paul turns immediately to people who judge the behavior in chapter 1 while doing comparable things themselves. Who is Paul describing here?
  3. Romans 2:17-29 addresses people who have religious identity — circumcision, the Law — without the reality behind it. Where do we see that same gap between religious identity and lived reality today?
  4. Romans 3:23 says "all" have sinned and fall short, with no exceptions. What difference does that make to how the rest of the letter can be read?

Leader note: It's tempting to let the group land quickly on "yes, everyone sins" as a safe, abstract conclusion. Paul's actual argument is more specific and more uncomfortable — he names the self-righteous religious insider (2:1-3, 17-29) as being in exactly the same position as the person they'd consider obviously guilty (1:18-32). If the conversation stays comfortably general, ask directly: which person in this passage is closest to us?

Close: Where might you personally still be assuming an exception applies to you? Sit with that in silence for a moment before anyone prays.

Session 2

Right With God, Not Because We Earned It

Romans 3:21–5:21

After three chapters proving no one measures up, Paul finally announces the solution: God's righteousness given, not earned, received by faith the same way Abraham received it centuries before the Law even existed.

Warm-up: Think of something you've received as a pure gift, with no way to pay it back. What did that feel like?

"Therefore, since we have been justified through faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ," — Romans 5:1, BSB

Discussion:

  1. Read Romans 3:21-26. What does it mean that God's righteousness is "apart from the Law" but still consistent with God's own justice (v. 26)?
  2. Read Romans 4:1-5. Why does Paul go back to Abraham — centuries before the Law was given — to make his case?
  3. Read Romans 5:1-5. Paul moves from being justified, to having peace with God, to — surprisingly — rejoicing in sufferings. Walk through that progression together: does it make emotional sense, or does it feel too fast?
  4. Read Romans 5:12-21, the Adam/Christ contrast. What is Paul claiming about the scope of what Christ undoes, compared to the scope of what Adam did?

Leader note: Romans 4 assumes familiarity with Genesis 15:6 ("Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness") — it may help to read that verse in Genesis first if anyone in the group doesn't know the reference Paul is building his whole argument on.

Close: Justification means being declared right with God, not gradually becoming good enough. Where is that distinction hardest for you to actually believe applies to you?

Session 3

Free, Not Because It's Easy

Romans 6–8

If grace covers sin, does sin stop mattering? Paul spends three chapters answering no — walking through the daily reality of the fight against sin in chapter 7, and landing on chapter 8, which Formation's inductive study guide calls "the climax of the entire letter."

Warm-up: What's a rule you followed for years before you understood the reason behind it — and what changed once you finally did?

"For I am convinced that neither death nor life, neither angels nor principalities, neither the present nor the future, nor any powers, neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord." — Romans 8:38-39, BSB

Discussion:

  1. Read Romans 6:1-4. Paul anticipates the objection "should we sin more so grace can increase?" and answers it before it's even fully asked. Why do you think that's the first thing he addresses right after chapter 5?
  2. Read Romans 7:14-25. Paul describes an internal war between wanting to do good and actually doing it. Does this match your own experience of the Christian life — why or why not?
  3. Read Romans 8:1-4. What does "no condemnation" actually remove, according to this passage — guilt, temptation, both, something else?
  4. Read Romans 8:38-39 together slowly. Which item on Paul's list feels most personally relevant to you right now?

Leader note: Chapter 7's "I do not do the good I want" (v. 19) can pull a group into a debate over whether Paul is describing a believer or an unbeliever — a genuinely disputed question among careful readers. Rather than settling it, it may be more useful to ask what's actually true of the group's own experience, since chapter 8's answer (no condemnation, life in the Spirit) applies either way.

Close: Read Romans 8:38-39 out loud together, as a group, as your closing prayer.

Session 4

God's Faithfulness, and a Life That Fits It

Romans 9–12 (9–16 to finish the letter)

Paul closes Romans by answering one more hard question — has God abandoned His promises to Israel? — before turning, in chapter 12, to what a right-with-God life actually looks like day to day. This session covers chapters 9-12 in full; chapters 13-16 are worth finishing on your own, since they cover specific practical instructions that don't need a full group session to unpack.

Warm-up: When has trusting God's timing been harder than trusting God's character?

"Therefore I urge you, brothers, on account of God's mercy, to offer your bodies as living sacrifices, holy and pleasing to God, which is your spiritual service of worship." — Romans 12:1, BSB

Discussion:

  1. Read Romans 9:1-5. What does Paul's grief over Israel tell you about how he wants this hard section read — as cold theology, or something else?
  2. Romans 9-11 wrestles with a genuinely disputed question — how God's election and human choice relate — that careful, sincere Christians read differently. Formation has a full article on this exact question at Predestination and Free Will if your group wants to go deeper after tonight. For now: what do chapters 9-11 agree on, regardless of how the harder question gets answered?
  3. Read Romans 11:17-24, the olive tree image. What warning does Paul give here to Gentile believers specifically?
  4. Read Romans 12:1-2. After eleven chapters of argument, Paul's first practical word is "therefore" — what is that "therefore" pointing back to?

Leader note: Chapters 9-11 are the part of Romans most likely to produce real disagreement in a group, since they touch predestination and election directly. That disagreement is fine and doesn't need to be resolved in one session — the goal here is understanding what Paul is actually arguing, not settling a centuries-old debate in one meeting.

Close: Name one specific, nameable way you want to "present your body as a living sacrifice" (12:1) this week — not abstractly, an actual action.
