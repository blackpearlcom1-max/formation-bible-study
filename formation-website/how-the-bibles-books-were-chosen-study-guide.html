Difficult Question

How the Bible's books were chosen — recognized, or decided?

Two verses apart, Paul tells Timothy he has known "the Holy Scriptures" since infancy, then declares that "all Scripture is God-breathed." Timothy was born roughly a decade after the resurrection — the only Scriptures he could have learned as a child were the Hebrew Bible, since the New Testament did not yet exist as a finished collection. That means the most-quoted verse for the authority of the whole Bible was written before the book it now sits inside had a table of contents. The tension, what different Christian traditions agree happened next, the documented history of how the books were recognized, and the question Formation doesn't answer for you.

schedule 13 min read · Topical Study · BSB Translation

The tension, stated plainly

"From infancy you have known the Holy Scriptures, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus." — 2 Timothy 3:15, BSB
"All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for instruction, for conviction, for correction, and for training in righteousness, so that the man of God may be complete, fully equipped for every good work." — 2 Timothy 3:16-17, BSB

Paul wrote these words to Timothy around AD 66-67, near the end of his own life, in what's traditionally counted as the last letter he wrote. Timothy's mother Eunice and grandmother Lois were Jewish believers who raised him in the Scriptures "from infancy" (2 Timothy 1:5), which puts his childhood roughly in the AD 30s and 40s — years before a single book of the New Testament had been written, let alone gathered into any collection. The "Holy Scriptures" a Jewish child could have known from infancy in that decade could only mean the Hebrew Bible. Two verses later, Paul makes the sweeping claim that "all Scripture is God-breathed" — a phrase now used to defend the inspired authority of the entire Bible, Old and New Testament together, sitting inside a letter that, by definition, wasn't yet part of the collection it seems to be describing. That's the real seed of the question: the text itself doesn't tell us how, or when, or by what process the 27 books that make up today's New Testament came to stand alongside the Hebrew Scriptures as "God-breathed." That history had to happen after Paul set down his pen, not because of an instruction embedded inside it.

What both threads actually agree on

Whatever settled the question eventually, the recognition of at least some New Testament writings as Scripture didn't wait for a council centuries later — it began inside the apostolic circle itself, within the lifetimes of the people who wrote the books.

Paul quotes a Gospel saying as "Scripture"

Years before he wrote 2 Timothy, Paul told Timothy: "For the Scripture says, 'Do not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain,' and, 'The worker is worthy of his wages'" (1 Timothy 5:18, BSB). The first quotation is Deuteronomy 25:4, straight from the Torah. The second matches, almost word for word, a saying of Jesus recorded in Luke 10:7. Paul introduces both with the identical formula — "the Scripture says" — placing a Gospel saying on the same footing as the Law, in a letter dated to around AD 62-64. No council or bishop authorized that move. Paul just made it, writing as an apostle to a co-worker he'd trained personally.

Peter puts Paul's letters in the same category

2 Peter goes further. Writing near the end of his own life, Peter refers to "our beloved brother Paul" and warns that untaught people distort his letters, "as they do the rest of the Scriptures, to their own destruction" (2 Peter 3:15-16, BSB). "The rest of the Scriptures" only makes sense if Paul's letters were already being counted among "the Scriptures" — the same label reserved for the Hebrew Bible. If this letter is genuinely Peter's, written in the traditional mid-to-late 60s AD window, that's one apostle formally placing another apostle's writings inside the category of Scripture while both men were still alive, or barely gone — not a decision imposed from outside the apostolic generation, but one made from inside it.

Put together, what the "recognized, not decided" camp and the "the Church determined it" camp actually agree on is this: the recognition of New Testament writings as Scripture-status starts on the New Testament's own pages, among the apostles themselves, decades before any list, council, or creed. The disagreement that remains isn't about whether that early recognition happened — it's about what, if anything, was still needed to finally close the list.

Two ways Christians have held this together

In the first reading, the councils and lists that show up later in church history didn't create the New Testament's authority — they ratified a consensus that had already substantially formed through generations of congregational use. Books were treated as self-authenticating: their apostolic origin, doctrinal consistency, and widespread reception across scattered, unconnected churches were what mattered, consistent with the internal evidence above of recognition beginning within the apostolic generation itself. On this reading, Athanasius of Alexandria's 39th Festal Letter (AD 367) — the earliest surviving document that names exactly today's 27 New Testament books, no more and no fewer — and the regional councils of Hippo (AD 393) and Carthage (AD 397) simply wrote down, formally, what believing communities had already been living out for decades.

A second reading points to something the first tends to underplay: real, extended, documented disagreement over specific books that lasted for centuries. The fourth-century historian Eusebius of Caesarea sorted New Testament writings into categories in his own history of the church — some universally "accepted," others "disputed" (the Greek term is antilegomena), including James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2 John, and 3 John. That means 2 Peter, the very letter used above as evidence of early recognition, was itself among the most contested books in the early church for a long stretch of its history — not a settled matter from day one. Given disagreement of that kind persisting at the highest levels of church scholarship for generations, this reading holds that a real exercise of teaching authority — not a rubber stamp on an already-obvious consensus — was needed to close the list. It's also the same reasoning behind why Catholic and Orthodox Old Testaments retain the Deuterocanonical books, affirmed at the Council of Trent in 1546, that Protestant Bibles — following the earlier, narrower Hebrew canon — do not include.

Both readings agree that the 27 books of the New Testament are the right ones, agree that recognition of at least some of them began early, inside the apostolic generation, and agree that the process took real time to reach its final, settled shape. What still divides them is whether the eventual closing of the list was a discovery of what had already quietly become true, or a decision the church was given genuine authority to make. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here.

The sourced evidence

Paul's letters were already circulating between churches

Colossians 4:16 instructs: "After this letter has been read among you, make sure that it is also read in the church of the Laodiceans, and that you in turn read the letter from Laodicea" (BSB). From very early in Paul's ministry, his letters weren't private mail — they were written to be read aloud in public worship and deliberately exchanged between congregations, the same function the Hebrew Scriptures already had in the synagogue. That circulation pattern is part of how apostolic writings began accumulating the kind of widespread, cross-congregational recognition both readings above depend on.

Nicaea didn't vote on the Bible

A popular claim, often repeated in novels and documentaries, is that Emperor Constantine or the Council of Nicaea in AD 325 voted on which books belong in the Bible. The surviving canons and creed from Nicaea don't support that claim — the council's actual business was the nature of Christ's relationship to God the Father, the controversy known as Arianism, not the canon of Scripture. As noted above, the earliest surviving document that names exactly today's 27 New Testament books is Athanasius's Festal Letter of AD 367 — four decades after Nicaea, and from an entirely separate context.

The most contested book was the one confirming the pattern

It's worth sitting with the irony directly: 2 Peter, quoted above as some of the earliest documented evidence for apostolic recognition of Scripture, was also — per Eusebius's own categorization — one of the New Testament's most disputed books, taking centuries longer than most others to reach the kind of universal acceptance it now describes happening to Paul's letters. The history of the canon isn't a story of instant, unanimous certainty followed by a rubber-stamp vote; it's a story of early, real recognition sitting alongside real, extended disagreement over the edges of the list, worked out over generations.

Questions it raises

  • If the recognition of at least some New Testament writings as Scripture began inside the apostolic generation itself (1 Timothy 5:18, 2 Peter 3:15-16), does that change how you weigh "the Church decided" against "the Church recognized" as the better description of what happened?
  • Eusebius's own fourth-century history lists 2 Peter, James, Jude, 2 John, and 3 John as long "disputed" before they gained wide acceptance. Does knowing that some books took centuries to be broadly received change how you read them today?
  • Protestant and Catholic/Orthodox Old Testaments differ over the Deuterocanonical books, a difference formalized at the Council of Trent in 1546. Reading 2 Timothy 3:15-17, 1 Timothy 5:18, 2 Peter 3:15-16, and Colossians 4:16 together, where do you find yourself landing on whether recognition or decision is the better word for how we got the Bible we have?
