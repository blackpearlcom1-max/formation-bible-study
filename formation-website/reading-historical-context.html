Bible Study Method

Reading in historical context.

Every passage was written to a real audience, in a real place, dealing with real circumstances we don't automatically share. Recovering that background isn't academic decoration — sometimes it's the difference between a popular misreading and what the text actually says.

schedule 8 min read · Intermediate · BSB Translation

Why background matters

Scripture wasn't written in a vacuum, and it wasn't written to us first. Paul's letters assume his readers already knew the local customs he referenced. John's letters to the seven churches in Revelation assume readers who knew each city's specific reputation, geography, and recent history. When we read those texts today, we're reading someone else's mail — genuinely meant for us, but originally addressed to people who shared context we don't automatically have.

Most of the time, the gap doesn't change much. But sometimes it changes everything, and there's no way to know which case you're in without doing the work of checking.

A repeatable process

1. Identify who wrote it, to whom, when, and why

A study Bible's introduction to a book usually covers this in a few paragraphs — author, audience, approximate date, and the occasion that prompted the writing. This is the fastest, highest-value step and takes only a few minutes.

2. Research any specific customs, places, or events the text references

If a passage mentions a specific city, custom, or practice, a study Bible's footnotes or a reputable background commentary can usually tell you what a first-century reader would have already known — without you needing to become a historian.

3. Consider the genre's conventions

A prophetic oracle, a personal letter, a piece of wisdom literature, and an apocalyptic vision each follow different conventions for how literally their imagery is meant to be taken. Reading any of them as if it were a different genre is its own kind of context error.

4. Separate the timeless principle from the culturally-specific vehicle

A passage's application may be tied to a specific first-century circumstance, while the underlying principle it teaches is not. Distinguishing which is which — rather than assuming the whole passage transfers unchanged, or that none of it does — is the actual interpretive work.

5. Apply the principle to today, not the ancient circumstance to today

Once the timeless principle is clear, apply that — not a woodenly literal replay of the first-century situation, which usually doesn't map onto modern life directly anyway.

Worked example: Laodicea's "lukewarm" church

I know your deeds; you are neither cold nor hot. How I wish you were one or the other! So because you are lukewarm—neither hot nor cold—I am about to vomit you out of My mouth!

Revelation 3:15–16, BSB.

This is one of the most quoted verses in Revelation, and it's usually read as a straightforward statement about enthusiasm: hot faith is good, lukewarm faith is worst of all — worse, apparently, than being outright cold. It's a memorable reading. Checking the historical background changes it substantially.

What Laodicea's water supply was actually like

Laodicea was a genuinely wealthy first-century city — wealthy enough that when an earthquake devastated it a few years before Revelation was written, the city rebuilt itself without requesting imperial financial aid, a point of local pride. But it had one real weakness: no local water source. Water arrived by aqueduct from two neighboring cities — hot mineral springs from Hierapolis, and cold, fresh water from Colossae. Both source cities' water was genuinely valued: Hierapolis's hot springs were known for their therapeutic, healing properties, and Colossae's cold water was prized as refreshing drinking water. By the time either supply traveled the aqueduct distance to Laodicea, though, it arrived neither hot nor cold — lukewarm, and carrying enough dissolved mineral content to taste unpleasant, even nauseating.

Why this reframes the passage

Read against that background, "hot" and "cold" in the passage aren't simply "enthusiastic" and "unenthusiastic," with lukewarm as a worse version of unenthusiastic. They're both pointing to something genuinely useful and valued — hot water that heals, cold water that refreshes — the way Hierapolis and Colossae's water actually was. "Lukewarm" isn't a lesser degree of either good thing; it's neither good thing. It's the specific, locally recognizable image of Laodicea's own unpleasant, mineral-tasting water — something you'd want to spit back out, not drink.

That shifts the passage's real target. It's less a warning about emotional temperature and more a warning about usefulness: the Laodicean church, like its city's water, had become neither genuinely healing nor genuinely refreshing to anyone — comfortable, self-sufficient (the text goes on to quote them saying "I am rich... and need nothing"), and functionally useless for the actual purpose water — or a church — exists to serve. That's a sharper, more specific rebuke than "be more excited," and it's only visible once the local water-supply background is in view.
