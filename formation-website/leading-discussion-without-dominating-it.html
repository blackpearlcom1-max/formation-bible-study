Group Leadership

Lead the discussion — don't deliver it.

The instinct that makes someone good at personal Bible study — having thought carefully about a passage before anyone else in the room has — is often the exact instinct that makes them a hard group leader to sit under. You've already got an answer, and it's tempting to just give it. This guide is about resisting that pull on purpose.

schedule 7 min read · Beginner · Leader Skill

The two ways this goes wrong

Almost every new leader errs in one of two directions, and both usually come from the same good instinct: caring enough to have actually prepared.

Over-leading turns every question into a mini-lecture. The leader has already worked out what the passage means, and when a member's answer is partial or a little off, the temptation is to fill in the rest right away. Do this enough times and the group learns, without anyone saying so out loud, that their job is to nod along until the leader gets to the real answer.

Under-leading looks like the opposite problem but has the same root cause. The leader asks a real question, the silence goes on a beat longer than feels comfortable, and — worried the question landed badly or the group is stuck — either rescues it with a leading hint or moves straight to the next question. Do this enough times and the group learns that if they just wait, someone will eventually do the thinking for them.

The core shift: your job is to ask, not answer

The most useful thing a leader brings to a discussion isn't the correct interpretation — it's the next question that gets the group closer to finding it themselves. That's a genuinely different skill than personal study, and it doesn't come from knowing the passage better. It comes from practicing restraint.

Even Jesus' most famous discussion in the Gospels follows this shape. At Caesarea Philippi, He doesn't open with the personal question. He starts general and low-stakes — "Who do people say the Son of Man is?" (Matthew 16:13) — and only after the disciples answer does He ask the one that actually matters: "But what about you?" He asked. "Who do you say I am?" (Matthew 16:15). The general question isn't padding on the way to the real one. It's what makes the personal one answerable at all — it gives the group something to react to before being asked to commit to their own view.

A repeatable process

1. Prepare open questions in advance, not just topics

"Let's talk about forgiveness" isn't a question — it's a topic, and topics invite silence because no one knows where to start. A prepared question ("What's made forgiveness genuinely hard for you personally?") does more work than an improvised one, because it was chosen in advance for what it invites, not tested live for what it produces.

2. Let silence sit before rescuing it

Count to ten in your head before saying anything else. Most groups need more processing time than feels comfortable to the person leading — a few seconds of quiet rarely means the question failed. A leader who fills every silence trains the group to wait it out rather than actually think it through.

3. Redirect instead of responding

When someone gives an answer, resist replying to it directly. "What do others think about that?" or "Does anyone see it differently?" keeps the conversation moving between members instead of bouncing back through the leader every single time — which is what quietly turns a discussion into a series of one-on-one conversations with an audience.

4. Follow up before moving on

"Say more about that" or "What do you mean by that?" gets more out of a shallow first answer than moving straight to the next question does — and it quietly signals that the group's actual thinking is what's being listened for, not just a right-sounding answer that lets everyone move along.

5. Handle a dominant voice privately, not publicly

If one person answers every question first, don't correct it in the room — that embarrasses them and teaches everyone else that speaking up is risky. A private word before the next meeting ("I love how engaged you are — could you hold back for the first minute so others get in first?") protects that person's dignity and the group's willingness to speak, at the same time.

Worked example: turning a shallow answer into real discussion

A group is discussing Ephesians 4:32.

Be kind and tenderhearted to one another, forgiving each other just as in Christ God forgave you.

Ephesians 4:32, BSB.

The leader asks, "What does this verse ask of us?" A member answers: "We should forgive people." Correct — and, on its own, not much of a discussion yet.

The over-leading response: the leader jumps in with a three-minute explanation of the theological basis of forgiveness, cross-referencing four other passages. Everything said is accurate. But by the time it's finished, the group has quietly shifted from participants into an audience — and the member who gave the first answer has learned that a short response gets replaced by the leader's longer one.

The better response: the leader follows up — "What's made that hard in one specific relationship, not in general?" That single question pulls the group out of restating the verse's content and into something much more textured: an actual person, an actual grudge, an actual difficulty applying "just as God forgave you" to a situation that doesn't feel resolved yet. The verse hasn't changed. The question did.

Practicing this in a Formation group

Formation's group discussion prompts — including the questions in this site's own group study guides — are written as open questions on purpose, not talking points. Using them as written, rather than mentally pre-answering them before the meeting starts, is itself one concrete way to practice the restraint this guide describes.
