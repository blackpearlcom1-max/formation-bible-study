Passage Study

John 1: the Word became flesh.

Fifty-one verses that open with poetry and end with a named man staring at a fig tree. The text, what a careful reading actually observes in it, the sourced evidence behind it, and where Formation deliberately doesn't take a side.

schedule 11 min read · All levels · BSB Translation

The text

In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was with God in the beginning. Through Him all things were made, and without Him nothing was made that has been made. In Him was life, and that life was the light of men. The Light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it.

There came a man who was sent from God. His name was John. He came as a witness to testify about the Light, so that through him everyone might believe. He himself was not the Light, but he came to testify about the Light. The true Light, who gives light to everyone, was coming into the world. He was in the world, and though the world was made through Him, the world did not recognize Him. He came to His own, and His own did not receive Him. But to all who did receive Him, to those who believed in His name, He gave the right to become children of God — children born not of blood, nor of the desire or will of man, but born of God.

The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us. We have seen His glory, the glory of the one and only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth. John testified concerning Him. He cried out, saying, "This is He of whom I said, 'He who comes after me has surpassed me because He was before me.'" From His fullness we have all received grace upon grace. For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. No one has ever seen God, but the one and only Son, who is Himself God and is at the Father's side, has made Him known.

And this was John's testimony when the Jews of Jerusalem sent priests and Levites to ask him, "Who are you?" He did not refuse to confess, but openly declared, "I am not the Christ." "Then who are you?" they inquired. "Are you Elijah?" He said, "I am not." "Are you the Prophet?" He answered, "No." So they said to him, "Who are you? We need an answer for those who sent us. What do you say about yourself?" John replied in the words of Isaiah the prophet: "I am a voice of one calling in the wilderness, 'Make straight the way for the Lord.'" Then the Pharisees who had been sent asked him, "Why then do you baptize, if you are not the Christ, nor Elijah, nor the Prophet?" "I baptize with water," John replied, "but among you stands One you do not know. He is the One who comes after me, the straps of whose sandals I am not worthy to untie." All this happened at Bethany beyond the Jordan, where John was baptizing.

The next day John saw Jesus coming toward him and said, "Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world! This is He of whom I said, 'A man who comes after me has surpassed me because He was before me.' I myself did not know Him, but the reason I came baptizing with water was that He might be revealed to Israel." Then John testified, "I saw the Spirit descending from heaven like a dove and resting on Him. I myself did not know Him, but the One who sent me to baptize with water told me, 'The man on whom you see the Spirit descend and rest is He who will baptize with the Holy Spirit.' I have seen and testified that this is the Son of God."

The next day John was there again with two of his disciples. When he saw Jesus walking by, he said, "Look, the Lamb of God!" And when the two disciples heard him say this, they followed Jesus. Jesus turned and saw them following. "What do you want?" He asked. They said to Him, "Rabbi" (which means Teacher), "where are You staying?" "Come and see," He replied. So they went and saw where He was staying, and spent that day with Him. It was about the tenth hour. Andrew, Simon Peter's brother, was one of the two who heard John's testimony and followed Jesus. He first found his brother Simon and told him, "We have found the Messiah" (which is translated as Christ). Andrew brought him to Jesus, who looked at him and said, "You are Simon son of John. You will be called Cephas" (which is translated as Peter).

The next day Jesus decided to set out for Galilee. Finding Philip, He told him, "Follow Me." Now Philip was from Bethsaida, the same town as Andrew and Peter. Philip found Nathanael and told him, "We have found the One Moses wrote about in the Law, the One the prophets foretold — Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph." "Can anything good come from Nazareth?" Nathanael asked. "Come and see," said Philip. When Jesus saw Nathanael approaching, He said of him, "Here is a true Israelite, in whom there is no deceit." "How do You know me?" Nathanael asked. Jesus replied, "Before Philip called you, I saw you under the fig tree." "Rabbi," Nathanael answered, "You are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel!" Jesus said to him, "Do you believe just because I told you I saw you under the fig tree? You will see greater things than these." Then He declared, "Truly, truly, I tell you, you will all see heaven open and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man."

John 1:1–51, BSB.

The observation

Read the first sentence again and notice what it's doing on purpose. "In the beginning" is the opening phrase of the entire Hebrew Bible — Genesis 1:1 — and John opens his account of Jesus with the identical phrase. That's not a coincidence a careful reader should skip past. Genesis 1 opens with "In the beginning God created." John 1 opens with "In the beginning was the Word." Same starting point, same timeless setting before creation, but a different grammatical subject — and the chapter spends its next seventeen verses explaining who that subject actually is, without using the name "Jesus" even once until verse 17.

Notice, too, that the chapter isn't one continuous scene — it moves through three distinct registers. Verses 1–18 are elevated, poetic, timeless: no dates, no locations, no dialogue, just declarations about the Word's nature and origin. Verses 19–34 shift hard into something concrete and datable: named officials (priests and Levites, Pharisees), a specific place (Bethany beyond the Jordan), and a real interrogation with back-and-forth questions and answers. Verses 35–51 narrow further still, into individually named people on specific consecutive days — "the next day" appears three times (29, 35, 43) — ending with one man, Nathanael, under one fig tree. The chapter's own structure moves from cosmic, to historical, to personal, in that order.

What's absent is worth noting too. The prologue never explains how the Word "became flesh" — it states the fact and moves on. And the chapter's climax isn't a theological statement at all; it's Nathanael's individual, surprised recognition after a private, specific, personal detail (being seen under a tree no one else was watching). The chapter that opens with the most sweeping claim in the whole Bible ends with one man's very small, very particular moment of being truly known.

The sourced evidence

Words worth pausing on

"Word" translates the Greek logos, a term loaded with meaning for both of John's likely first-century audiences at once. In Jewish tradition, God's word and God's wisdom were already spoken of as active, almost personal agents present at creation — Proverbs 8 pictures Wisdom "beside Him" as "a master craftsman" when God laid the world's foundations, and Aramaic translations of the Hebrew Bible in use around this period regularly used memra ("word") as a way of speaking about God's own presence and action without over-familiarity. In Greek philosophical tradition, logos was already a technical term — the rational principle Stoic philosophers believed ordered and ran the entire universe. John's opening line uses a word both audiences would recognize, then makes a claim neither tradition had made on its own: this logos is a person, and this person "became flesh."

Two other terms matter here. "One and only" (v. 14, 18) translates monogenēs, a word describing a unique relationship — an only child, in context — rather than necessarily describing derived origin. And "made His dwelling" (v. 14) translates a Greek verb built on the word for "tent" (skēnē) — literally, "pitched His tent" or "tabernacled." That's a direct echo of Exodus 40, where God's glory filled the tabernacle, the tent where His presence dwelt among Israel in the wilderness. John is making a specific, locatable claim: what the tabernacle was for Israel, the Word made flesh now is.

What John 1 was written into

John's first readers lived in a world where "Logos" already meant something specific in Greek philosophy (the ordering principle of the cosmos, as in Stoic thought) and where Jewish wisdom literature had already personified God's word and wisdom as present with Him before creation (Proverbs 8:22–31; compare also the wider Wisdom tradition in books like Sirach). John's opening doesn't reject either vocabulary — he writes in a way both a Greek-educated reader and a Torah-literate reader could recognize something familiar in. What he adds, that neither tradition supplies on its own, is verse 14: this ordering, wisdom-bearing Word didn't just act in the world or speak through it — it became a specific human being, with a specific dwelling, at a specific point in datable history.

A structure that argues its own case

The chapter's movement from timeless prologue (1–18) to dated, interrogated testimony (19–34) to individually named callings on consecutive days (35–51) isn't just good storytelling craft — it enacts the very claim the prologue makes. A chapter about the eternal Word entering real, particular, dated history is itself built to move from the eternal and general into the dated and particular. The form matches the content.

Questions it raises

  • Verses 12–13 say that those who received the Word and believed in His name were given the right to become children of God — "children born... of God." Sincere, Bible-believing Christians have long disagreed about the order implied here: whether this new birth is something God does first, enabling a person to believe, or whether it's the result that follows from believing. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here. But both sides of that disagreement agree on what the verse plainly states either way: this new status isn't inherited, achieved, or granted by any human decision — "not of blood, nor of the desire or will of man." What do you think changes, practically, about how a person lives depending on which view they hold?
  • John's opening line uses language that a Greek philosophical reader and a Torah-literate Jewish reader would each recognize from their own tradition, then adds a claim (v. 14) that neither tradition had made before. Scholars still debate how intentionally John was engaging Greek philosophy versus primarily drawing on Jewish wisdom literature, or something distinct from both. Does it change how you read the chapter to imagine either audience — or both at once — hearing this for the first time?
  • The chapter withholds the name "Jesus" for seventeen verses, describing Him instead as the Word, the Light, and the Life. By the time individual people meet Him in verses 35–51, they arrive at names and titles of their own — Rabbi, Messiah, Son of God, King of Israel — each one earned through an actual encounter, not handed to them in advance. What would it look like to let your own understanding of who Jesus is be built the same way — through encounter first, description second — rather than the other way around?
