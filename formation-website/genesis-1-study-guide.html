Passage Study

Genesis 1: in the beginning, God created.

Thirty-one verses, six days, one repeating rhythm — and centuries of questions this chapter has generated ever since. The text, what a careful reading actually observes in it, the sourced literary and historical evidence behind it, and where Formation deliberately doesn't take a side.

schedule 10 min read · All levels · BSB Translation

The text

In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth. Now the earth was formless and void, and darkness was over the surface of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the surface of the waters.

And God said, "Let there be light," and there was light. And God saw that the light was good, and He separated the light from the darkness. God called the light "day," and the darkness He called "night." And there was evening, and there was morning — the first day.

And God said, "Let there be an expanse between the waters, to separate the waters from the waters." So God made the expanse and separated the waters beneath it from the waters above. And it was so. God called the expanse "sky." And there was evening, and there was morning — the second day.

And God said, "Let the waters under the sky be gathered into one place, so that the dry land may appear." And it was so. God called the dry land "earth," and the gathering of waters He called "seas." And God saw that it was good. Then God said, "Let the earth bring forth vegetation: seed-bearing plants and fruit trees, each bearing fruit with seed according to its kind." And it was so. The earth produced vegetation: seed-bearing plants according to their kinds and trees bearing fruit with seed according to their kinds. And God saw that it was good. And there was evening, and there was morning — the third day.

And God said, "Let there be lights in the expanse of the sky to distinguish between the day and the night, and let them be signs to mark the seasons and days and years. And let them serve as lights in the expanse of the sky to shine upon the earth." And it was so. God made two great lights: the greater light to rule the day and the lesser light to rule the night. And He made the stars as well. God set these lights in the expanse of the sky to shine upon the earth, to preside over the day and the night, and to separate the light from the darkness. And God saw that it was good. And there was evening, and there was morning — the fourth day.

And God said, "Let the waters teem with living creatures, and let birds fly above the earth in the open expanse of the sky." So God created the great sea creatures and every living thing that moves, with which the waters teemed according to their kinds, and every winged bird after its kind. And God saw that it was good. Then God blessed them and said, "Be fruitful and multiply and fill the waters of the seas, and let birds multiply on the earth." And there was evening, and there was morning — the fifth day.

And God said, "Let the earth bring forth living creatures according to their kinds: livestock, land crawlers, and beasts of the earth according to their kinds." And it was so. God made the beasts of the earth according to their kinds, the livestock according to their kinds, and everything that crawls upon the earth according to its kind. And God saw that it was good. Then God said, "Let Us make man in Our image, after Our likeness, to rule over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air, over the livestock, and over all the earth itself and every creature that crawls upon it." So God created man in His own image; in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them. God blessed them and said to them, "Be fruitful and multiply, and fill the earth and subdue it; rule over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air and every creature that crawls upon the earth." Then God said, "Behold, I have given you every seed-bearing plant on the face of all the earth, and every tree whose fruit contains seed. They will be yours for food. And to every beast of the earth and every bird of the air and every creature that crawls upon the earth — everything that has the breath of life in it — I have given every green plant for food." And it was so. And God looked upon all that He had made, and indeed, it was very good. And there was evening, and there was morning — the sixth day.

Genesis 1:1–31, BSB.

The observation

Read it once more and watch the rhythm. Six times, almost the same five beats repeat: God speaks ("And God said"), the command is given, the command is carried out ("and it was so"), God evaluates the result ("and God saw that it was good"), and a time stamp closes the day ("there was evening, and there was morning"). It's a chapter built like a refrain, not a freeform narrative — and refrains are worth noticing precisely where they break. The sixth day is the one place the pattern visibly stretches: instead of a simple command, the text shifts to deliberation ("Let Us make man in Our image"), the usual "and it was so" doesn't appear in the same spot, and a blessing and direct address get added ("God blessed them and said to them") that nothing earlier in the chapter received.

Look also at how the six days are grouped, not just how they're paced. Days one through three build three spaces — light and dark, sky and sea, land and vegetation. Days four through six then fill each of those same three spaces, in the same order — sun/moon/stars fill the light and dark, birds and sea creatures fill the sky and sea, land animals and humans fill the land. That's not incidental structure; it's a matched set, built in two passes over the same three domains.

Now notice what's absent. There's no resistance anywhere in the chapter — no negotiation, no struggle, nothing pushing back on the command. Every single act ends the same way: "and it was so." And when the text does slow down to mark something as different, it isn't marking size or complexity — the sea creatures and land animals are also called "living creatures" — it's marking address. Nothing else in Genesis 1 is spoken of as being made "in Our image, after Our likeness." That language is reserved for one moment, in one verse, for one creature.

The sourced evidence

Words worth pausing on

The chapter actually uses two different Hebrew verbs for God's creative action, and the alternation isn't random. Bara ("created") appears at the universe's opening act (1:1), again when conscious sea and air life first appears (1:21), and then three times in a single verse for humanity (1:27) — a repetition the text gives to nothing else. In between, ordinary forming work — the expanse (1:7), the sun and moon (1:16), the land animals (1:25) — uses a more general word, asah ("made"). Whatever else it means, the pattern itself is a textual fact: the chapter reserves its strongest word for three specific moments, and humanity gets triple emphasis that even the sea and sky's first living creatures don't.

Two other terms are worth knowing. Tohu wa-bohu — "formless and void" (1:2) — describes the earth's unorganized starting condition, before any of the six days' work begins. And tselem and demut — "image" and "likeness" (1:26–27) — are the two words the chapter uses, side by side, to describe whatever relationship humanity has to God that nothing else in the chapter shares.

What Genesis 1 was written against

Ancient Near Eastern creation accounts that predate or run alongside Genesis — the Babylonian Enuma Elish is the best documented — typically describe the world's origin as the byproduct of conflict between rival gods, with humanity created afterward as an afterthought, often explicitly as forced labor for the gods' benefit. Genesis 1 reads like a direct answer to that picture, whether or not it was written as one: there is no rival power here, no battle, nothing this God has to overcome. He speaks, and it happens. The sun and moon, deities in most surrounding cultures, are here demoted to "lights" with a job description — marking seasons, days, and years — rather than powers to be worshiped. And instead of an afterthought serving the gods' needs, humanity is the chapter's final, most emphasized act, blessed and given responsibility rather than servitude.

A structure that argues its own case

Independent of how any reader dates or paces the six days, the chapter's construction is deliberate: "good" or "very good" appears seven times, each of the six days closes with the identical evening-and-morning formula, and the two three-day sets correspond space-for-space. That's not the shape of a random or careless retelling — it's the shape of something composed with real intention, verse by verse, toward a specific point about who is doing this and why it matters.

Questions it raises

  • Sincere, Bible-believing Christians read the length and pace of these six days differently — a literal 24-hour sequence, a literary framework organizing creation into matching pairs, a sequence of longer ages, and other views besides. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here. But setting it aside entirely: does anything the text itself actually emphasizes — who is doing the creating, and why humanity gets a different kind of introduction than everything else in the chapter — depend on resolving it first?
  • The chapter marks humanity's creation with its strongest verb, repeated three times in one verse, and with language ("image," "likeness") given to nothing else in the chapter. Readers across traditions have taken that to mean different things — a capacity to reason and relate, a function of ruling and representing God on His behalf, or something else. What do you think the text is claiming is genuinely unprecedented about humanity's appearance, and what would change about how you read the rest of the chapter depending on the answer?
  • Every single day in this chapter ends with the same three words: "and it was so." Immediate, complete compliance, six times over — nothing in creation ever resists, delays, or negotiates what it's told. What would it actually look like to live inside that pattern yourself, rather than just admire it from the outside?
