Book Guide

Understand Ephesians.

Six chapters, almost perfectly split in half: three chapters of doctrine — who you are in Christ — followed by three chapters of practice — how that identity works out in a marriage, a household, a workplace, and a spiritual battle. It's the clearest single statement in Paul's letters of the pattern behind all of them: belief first, behavior second.

schedule 11 min read · Beginner to Intermediate · Berean Standard Bible

Who wrote it, from where, and to whom

Paul wrote Ephesians around AD 60–62, during the same Roman imprisonment as Philippians and Colossians (Acts 28:16, 30–31) — these three letters are usually grouped together as the "Prison Epistles." One genuine textual detail is worth knowing going in: the earliest and most reliable Greek manuscripts of Ephesians 1:1 lack the phrase "in Ephesus," which has led most scholars to conclude this was likely a circular letter, meant to be copied and passed among several churches in the Roman province of Asia (of which Ephesus, its most prominent city, was probably the first stop). That's a manuscript observation, not a doctrinal dispute — it doesn't change anything about the letter's content, just its original delivery.

Unlike Philippians, which responds to a specific occasion (a financial gift, a leadership dispute), Ephesians reads less like a reply to particular news and more like a full, unhurried summary of Paul's gospel — who God is, what Christ accomplished, who the church now is, and how that church should live.

The structure: three chapters of doctrine, three of practice

Ephesians splits almost exactly down the middle. The pivot point is 4:1, where Paul writes "I urge you to walk in a manner worthy of the calling you have received" — everything before that verse establishes what the calling is; everything after it explains what walking worthy of it looks like.

Ch. 1Chosen, Sealed, Blessed

A single long sentence in the Greek (1:3–14) lists what believers already have in Christ — chosen before the foundation of the world, adopted, redeemed, sealed with the Holy Spirit. Paul then prays that the Ephesians would grasp the scale of what they've been given (1:15–23).

Ch. 2Grace, and a Wall Torn Down

The theological center of the letter — see below. Dead in sin, made alive by grace through faith (2:1–10), then the specific, historic hostility between Jew and Gentile abolished in Christ (2:11–22).

Ch. 3The Mystery Revealed

Paul describes his own calling to explain this "mystery" — that Gentiles are now "fellow heirs" with Israel (3:1–13) — then closes the doctrinal half with a prayer for the Ephesians' inner strength and a doxology many churches still use today (3:14–21).

Ch. 4One Body, Growing Up

The pivot verse (4:1) opens the practical half. A call to unity built on a sevenfold "one" (4:1–6), gifts given to equip the church toward maturity (4:7–16), and the first concrete instruction: put off the old self, put on the new (4:17–32).

Ch. 5Walk in Love, Light, and Wisdom

Practical holiness (5:1–20) leads into the household code's first and most-discussed pairing: instructions for wives and husbands, framed around Christ's own self-giving love for the church (5:21–33).

Ch. 6Household, and the Armor of God

The household code continues — children and fathers, servants and masters (6:1–9) — before the letter's famous closing image: the full armor of God, for a struggle Paul is careful to name correctly. See the worked example below.

Grace, and a wall torn down (2:8–18)

This chapter does two things, and the connection between them is easy to miss if you only ever quote the first half on its own.

"For it is by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves; it is the gift of God, not by works, so that no one can boast. For we are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance as our way of life." — Ephesians 2:8–10 (BSB)

Verses 8–9 are quoted constantly, and rightly — grace, not works, received through faith, leaving no room for boasting. But verse 10 is part of the same sentence, not a separate thought: those who are saved by grace are also "created in Christ Jesus to do good works." Grace isn't opposed to good works; it's what produces them. The order matters — workmanship, then works, not the reverse.

Paul then applies that same grace to a specific historical wound: the hostility between Jews and Gentiles.

"For He Himself is our peace, who has made the two one and has torn down the dividing wall of hostility... He did this to create in Himself one new man out of the two, thus making peace, and reconciling both of them to God in one body through the cross, by which He put to death their hostility." — Ephesians 2:14–16 (BSB)

The "dividing wall" is a real, physical detail Paul's original readers would have recognized instantly: the temple in Jerusalem had an actual stone barrier (archaeologists have recovered inscribed warning plaques from it) that Gentiles were forbidden, on pain of death, to cross into the inner courts. Paul takes that concrete, familiar wall and says Christ tore down its spiritual equivalent — not a general feeling of unity, but the specific, centuries-old division between these two peoples, reconciled into "one new man."

Worked example: Ephesians 6:10–13, observed and interpreted

"Finally, be strong in the Lord and in His mighty power. Put on the full armor of God, so that you can make your stand against the devil's schemes. For our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the powers of this world's darkness, and against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly realms." — Ephesians 6:10–13 (BSB)

Observation: Before Paul names a single piece of armor, he first names what the fight is not against: "flesh and blood." Only after that explicit denial does he name what it actually is against — rulers, authorities, spiritual forces. The armor list that follows (belt, breastplate, shoes, shield, helmet, sword) is famous, but it's downstream of this correction, not the starting point.

Interpretation: "Flesh and blood" is a way of naming actual people — the coworker, the family member, the person on the other side of a disagreement. Paul isn't saying conflict with people never happens; he's saying that treating a person as the real enemy misdiagnoses the fight. The armor is defensive gear for a spiritual struggle, not equipment for a personal one — which is also why every named piece (truth, righteousness, the gospel, faith, salvation, the word of God) is something you put on yourself, not something you aim at somebody else.

Application: This is a genuinely practical redirect, not just a nice observation. The next time a person feels like the enemy in a real conflict, verse 12 is worth returning to directly: is the actual struggle here spiritual, and is the response Paul describes — armor worn, not weapons aimed — the one being reached for?

Study Ephesians in Formation

Because Ephesians divides so cleanly into doctrine (1–3) and practice (4–6), Formation's color-coding system is well-suited to marking that seam directly — color every occurrence of "in Christ" or "in Him" through chapters 1–3, then switch to marking every imperative ("walk," "put on," "put off") from 4:1 onward, and the letter's own structure becomes visible on the page. Ask the Word's cross-reference mode can also place the armor of God (6:10–17) alongside its Old Testament sources in Isaiah 11:5, 52:7, and 59:17, so you can see how deliberately Paul is drawing on that older imagery rather than inventing it from nothing.
