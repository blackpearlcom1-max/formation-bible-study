Difficult Question

Why do bad things happen to people who don't deserve them?

Job's friends assumed his catastrophic losses meant hidden sin. Jesus rejected that exact logic twice — for a man born blind, and for victims of a collapsed tower. Read together, the Bible refuses one question and answers a different one. The tension, what both threads agree on, the sourced evidence, and the question Formation doesn't answer for you.

schedule 10 min read · Topical Study · BSB Translation

The tension, stated plainly

"Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" Jesus answered, "Neither this man nor his parents sinned, but this happened so that the works of God would be displayed in him." — John 9:2-3, BSB
"Cursed is the ground because of you; through toil you will eat of it all the days of your life." — Genesis 3:17, BSB

Set side by side, these two passages seem to pull in opposite directions. In John 9, Jesus is handed the oldest theological reflex on record — something terrible happened, so whose sin caused it? — and he refuses the premise outright, on the record, for a man who had been blind since birth. But Genesis 3 is just as direct that suffering itself, in general, entered human experience precisely because of sin: the ground is cursed, ordinary work becomes toil, and death follows close behind. One passage says stop looking for the sin behind this specific suffering. Another says sin is exactly why suffering exists at all. Both are in the same Bible, both are meant to be taken at face value, and neither one quietly cancels the other.

What both threads actually agree on

The book of Job settles the individual question before Job's three friends ever open their mouths to argue the opposite. After losing his flocks, his servants, and all ten of his children in a single day, "Job did not sin or charge God with wrongdoing" (1:22). After a second wave of suffering — a body covered in sores — the verdict repeats: "In all this, Job did not sin in what he said" (2:10). Job's specific catastrophe is never once traced by the book itself to something Job did. Yet the book never denies that suffering is real, or that it happens inside a world where sin operates broadly and often violently — Sabean raiders, a fire, a wind, a collapsed house are all named plainly, with no softening. What the book refuses is the leap from the general fact (sin has broken the world) to the specific accusation (this man's suffering must mean this man sinned).

Luke 13 makes the same move even more directly, and Jesus makes it twice in five verses, using nearly identical language each time. Told about Galileans whom Pilate had killed, mixing their blood with their own sacrifices, Jesus asks the crowd's unspoken question out loud — were they worse sinners than other Galileans, "because they suffered this way"? — and answers it himself: "No, I tell you" (13:2-3). He then raises a second disaster the crowd would have known firsthand, eighteen people killed when a tower in Siloam collapsed on them, asks whether they were "more sinful than all the others living in Jerusalem," and gives the identical answer again: "No, I tell you" (13:4-5). Two disasters, two audiences primed to assume the victims had it coming, two flat denials — paired, notably, with a call to repentance that applies to everyone listening, not a verdict on anyone already dead.

Romans 8 agrees from the opposite direction, and just as plainly refuses to minimize what it's describing. "I consider that our present sufferings are not comparable to the glory that will be revealed in us" (8:18) assumes real, present, worth-naming suffering — not suffering the text is embarrassed to admit. The whole of creation, Paul writes, "has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until the present time" (8:22). Nothing here is denying that the world is genuinely broken. It's locating the brokenness at the level of the whole created order, not at the level of a single sufferer's private ledger.

Two different questions, easy to collapse into one

"Why does suffering exist at all?" and "why did this happen to me, specifically?" sound like the same question asked at different volumes. Scripture treats them as genuinely different. The first question gets a direct answer, traced to a real event: Genesis 3's curse on the ground, Romans 8's creation "subjected to futility." The second question gets refused, consistently, every time it's raised as an accusation against a particular sufferer — Job by his friends, the blind man by the disciples, the Galileans and the Siloam victims by the crowd around Jesus. Job's three friends, Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, and Zophar the Naamathite (2:11), spend the rest of the book collapsing these two questions into one, insisting Job's specific suffering must trace back to some specific, hidden sin. The book's own opening two chapters — which none of the three friends ever hear — have already told the reader they're wrong before they say a word.

A structural word next to a moral one

The Greek verb behind "sinned" in John 9:2-3, hēmarten, describes a specific act committed by a specific person — exactly the kind of thing the disciples were hunting for in the blind man's history. Romans 8:20's word for the state creation was subjected to, mataiotēti ("futility"), describes a structural condition, not an act — nobody's personal hēmarten put creation there; Paul says plainly it was subjected "not by its own will, but because of the One who subjected it, in hope" (8:20). Meanwhile the Hebrew word behind Job's own description in 1:1, tam ("blameless," sometimes rendered "complete" or "of integrity"), is a moral verdict about Job specifically — one the text delivers before any of his suffering starts, and repeats without qualification after all of it lands. Three different words, describing three different things: a specific act, a structural condition, and one man's specific character. Confusing the second for the first is exactly the mistake Job's friends make for the length of an entire book.

Two ways Christians have held this together

The free will and fallen world reading

On this reading, most suffering is adequately explained by two things working together: real human free will (people harm each other, and choices carry real consequences), and a natural order that Romans 8:20-22 and Genesis 3:17-19 both describe as groaning under decay, thorns, and death since the Fall. This reading doesn't need to trace any single tragedy back to a single sinner's private guilt — the "subjected to futility" world produces disease, disaster, and decline broadly, the way a cursed ground produces "thorns and thistles" (Genesis 3:18) broadly, without a specific transaction behind each specific outcome.

Job's own answer: trust without an explanation

A second reading takes its cue from what the book of Job conspicuously withholds. Job himself is never let in on the conversation between God and the Accuser that opens the book — the reader knows the reason for Job's suffering; Job never does. On this reading, some suffering isn't meant to be explained to the sufferer at all, only trusted through. Job's own recorded response becomes the model, not a private theory about causation: "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked I will return. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the name of the LORD" (1:21) — worship and trust offered before any explanation arrives, rather than an explanation that produces trust afterward.

Both readings agree that hunting for a specific sin behind a specific tragedy is the wrong instinct — the one Job's friends modeled and the one Jesus refused twice in Luke 13. What still divides them is a real question about how much explanatory weight the fallen-world framework can bear versus how much of what happens to any one person is simply not going to be explained this side of the story's ending. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here.

The sourced evidence

An ancient assumption the book of Job was built to dismantle

Job's three friends — Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, and Zophar the Naamathite, all introduced together in 2:11 — represent a widely shared ancient Near Eastern assumption: prosperity is divine reward, suffering is divine punishment, without exception. The book's own opening two chapters, which the friends never hear, have already stipulated the opposite before the friends' speeches even begin: a man the narrator twice calls "blameless and upright" (1:1, repeated by God Himself in 1:8) loses everything not because of anything he did, but because of a heavenly conversation he himself never learns about.

A second controversy hiding inside the healing

John's Gospel adds a detail almost in passing: the healing in John 9 happened on a Sabbath. That single fact turns a story that began as a referendum on the blind man's family history into a second, separate controversy — a dispute among the Pharisees themselves over whether someone willing to heal on a Sabbath could possibly be "from God," since the same chapter records them split over exactly that question. The man born blind ends up on trial twice in one chapter: once, before he could speak for himself, over whose sin explained his blindness; and again, after he could, over whether the God who reversed it was legitimate at all.

The same active-working language, describing hardship from two sides

John 9:3 ties the blind man's whole ordeal to a purpose: "so that the works of God would be displayed in him." Romans 8:28 uses a related idea from the other end of a hard chapter: "God works all things together for the good of those who love Him." Neither verse is offered as an explanation that cancels the hardship it sits beside — the blind man was still blind for however many years before this chapter, and Romans 8's "all things" arrives immediately after, not instead of, a full paragraph on creation's groaning. Both verses locate God's active work inside the suffering they're describing, not as a substitute for it.

Questions it raises

  • Job's friends and the disciples in John 9 both reached for the same instinct: something bad happened, so whose sin explains it? When something goes wrong in your own life, or someone else's, is that the first question you reach for too?
  • Genesis 3 and Romans 8 both describe suffering as something built into a fallen world broadly, not measured out to specific people for specific offenses. Does holding both of those at once — a genuinely fallen world, and no one-to-one scorekeeping inside it — change how you'd respond to someone in the middle of a hard season?
  • Whether unexplained suffering is best met with the free-will-and-fallen-world explanation, or with Job's own posture of trust offered before any explanation arrives, is a real, long-held difference among sincere Christians. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here — but reading Job, John 9, and Luke 13 side by side, where do you find yourself landing, and why?
