Bible Study Method

Bible journaling without the blank page.

A blank notebook and a verse is where most journaling habits go to die. Not because the idea is bad — because "write something" isn't actually a method. Here's a structure that turns a blank page into four honest questions.

schedule 6 min read · Beginner · BSB Translation

Journaling is a record, not a technique

Every other method guide on this site teaches a specific technique you apply to a specific passage in a single sitting — observe and interpret it, trace a theme through it, look up its Greek. Bible journaling is different in kind, not just in steps. It isn't a technique for getting more out of one passage; it's a habit of keeping a written record of your walk with Scripture over time, so that six months from now you can see not just what a verse says, but what it's meant to you across a season of reading it.

That distinction matters because it changes what "doing it wrong" looks like. You can't really fail at journaling the way you can misread a passage — but you can absolutely lose the habit to a blank page that offers no starting point. The fix isn't more discipline. It's a structure simple enough to return to on a tired Tuesday.

How this differs from SOAP

If you've read the SOAP Method guide, this might sound familiar — and it should, because both are lightweight daily habits rather than full inductive study sessions. The difference is what each is optimized for. SOAP is a fixed four-step template applied fresh to a new verse each day, built to end in one specific written prayer. Journaling is looser and more personal: some entries will be a single line, some will run half a page, and the point isn't a consistent format but an honest, dated record you can look back through — noticing what you were wrestling with in March, or which verse you've now written about three separate times without realizing it.

Use SOAP when you want a repeatable daily rhythm. Use journaling when you want the freedom to write differently depending on what a passage actually stirs up that day — and a growing record to look back on.

A structure for the blank page

You don't need all four every time. On a rushed morning, one honest sentence under "What stood out" is a real entry. The structure exists to give you somewhere to start, not a checklist to complete.

1. What stood out

A word, phrase, or image that caught your attention — before you explain why. Just name it. This is the same first move as observation in inductive study, but journaling doesn't require you to be systematic about it.

2. What it means, as best you understand it right now

Not a final answer — a snapshot of your current understanding, dated. Coming back to this line a year later and seeing how your understanding has deepened or shifted is one of the real payoffs of keeping a journal at all.

3. Where this shows up in your actual life right now

Specific, not general. Not "I should trust God more" but the actual situation this week where that trust is being tested. This is what makes an entry worth rereading later — it's tied to a real moment, not a floating principle.

4. A question you're still sitting with

Not every passage resolves cleanly, and pretending it does trains you to skip past real tension. Writing down the actual open question — and dating it — lets you notice, months later, whether it ever got answered.

Worked example: Psalm 119:15-16

I will meditate on Your precepts and regard Your ways. I will delight in Your statutes; I will not forget Your word.

Psalm 119:15–16, BSB.

An entry using the four-part structure — journaling entries don't need to be long, and this one intentionally isn't:

What stood out: The psalmist names four separate actions in two verses — meditate, regard, delight, not forget. That's not one vague posture toward Scripture, it's four distinct things happening at once.

What it means, as best I understand it right now: "Meditate" and "regard" are about attention — actually thinking something through, not skimming it. "Delight" is different — it's the emotional response, finding real pleasure in it rather than treating it as duty. "Not forget" is about retention over time, which is honestly the one I'm worst at. I can read something and feel the delight in the moment and still lose it by next week.

Where this shows up in my life right now: I had a real moment of clarity reading Romans 12 two weeks ago — and I genuinely can't remember now what specifically struck me. That's the "not forget" gap in action, not a hypothetical.

A question I'm still sitting with: Is journaling itself basically my practical answer to "I will not forget" — a structural fix for a real memory gap — or am I just replacing genuine retention with a paper trail I never reread? Worth checking back on in six months.

Notice what that entry does that a single "I liked this verse" line wouldn't: it names something specific in the text, states a real (not final) understanding, ties it to an actual moment, and ends with an honest open question instead of a tidy conclusion. That's the whole method — repeated, dated, and reread occasionally rather than written once and forgotten.
