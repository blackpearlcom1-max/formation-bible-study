Bible Study Method

Word studies, without the root fallacy.

Looking up a Greek or Hebrew word feels like doing real scholarship. But the single most common way word studies go wrong isn't looking something up incorrectly — it's assuming a word's origin tells you its meaning. Here's the difference, and a process that avoids it.

schedule 8 min read · Intermediate · BSB Translation

This pairs with the Greek Interlinear guide — but isn't the same thing

The Greek Interlinear for Beginners guide on this site teaches the mechanics: how to open an interlinear tool, find the Greek word behind an English translation, and read a basic lexicon entry. This guide is about something different — the actual discipline of turning that lookup into a responsible conclusion about what a word means. You can use an interlinear correctly and still walk away with a wrong conclusion, and there's one specific mistake that causes this more than any other.

The root fallacy

The root fallacy is the assumption that a word's history — its root, its etymology, what it's built from — determines its meaning wherever it's used. It feels like it should be true. It usually isn't. Words drift. A word's actual meaning in a given sentence is set by how it's actually used, by that author, in that context — not by where it originally came from.

English has the same problem. "Nice" comes from a Latin root meaning "ignorant." "Awful" once meant "full of awe." If you explained a modern sentence using either word's original root meaning, you'd get it backwards. The same risk applies to Greek and Hebrew — except it's easier to miss, because looking up an ancient root feels rigorous even when it's leading you the wrong direction.

A repeatable process

1. Identify the actual word behind the translation

Use an interlinear to find the specific Greek or Hebrew word an English translation represents — this is the mechanical step the Interlinear guide covers.

2. Check the word's range of usage, not just its origin

Most words in Scripture — like most words in any language — carry a range of related meanings depending on context, not one fixed meaning. A lexicon entry that lists several possible senses is showing you that range, not offering you a menu to pick from arbitrarily.

3. See how the same word is used elsewhere in Scripture

A concordance lets you see every other place a word appears. Reading several of those other uses — especially by the same author — is what actually narrows down which sense fits your passage, far more reliably than the word's etymology does.

4. Notice when different English translations disagree

When several respected translations render the same word differently, that's not noise — it's a signal that real translators saw genuine interpretive nuance in that spot. Worth investigating, not averaging away.

5. Let the immediate context decide, not the root

After steps 2–4 give you the word's real range, the specific sentence it's sitting in — not the word's origin story — is what tells you which sense the author meant.

Worked example: the "power" in Romans 1:16

I am not ashamed of the gospel, because it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, first to the Jew, then to the Greek.

Romans 1:16, BSB.

This is one of the most frequently mistaught word studies in popular Bible teaching, which makes it a useful cautionary example. The Greek word behind "power" is dynamis (δύναμις, Strong's 1411) — and yes, it's the root of the English word "dynamite." A very common teaching move takes this connection and runs with it: the gospel is "dynamite," an explosive, detonating force. It's a vivid image. It's also backwards.

Why the "dynamite" reading fails

Dynamite the explosive didn't exist until 1867, when Alfred Nobel coined the name from the Greek word — roughly 1,800 years after Paul wrote Romans. Paul could not have meant "explosive" by dynamis, because the association between that word and an explosion didn't exist yet. The etymology runs in exactly the wrong direction: dynamite borrowed its name from dynamis's existing meaning of "power" and "ability" — it isn't evidence that dynamis itself ever meant something violent or explosive. This is the root fallacy in its most textbook form: a real linguistic connection, pointed the wrong way in time, producing a meaning the original word never had.

What dynamis actually means, by usage

Dynamis appears about 120 times in the New Testament, and its range of usage is broad but consistent: it's fundamentally about inherent ability or capacity to act — not sudden violent force. Checking a spread of its other appearances shows the range clearly: Jesus's miracles are called dynameis (Matthew 11:20-21) — "mighty works," not explosions. A servant is entrusted with money "according to his own ability [dynamin]" in the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:15) — plainly about capacity, not force. The word even describes the "meaning" or force of a spoken expression (1 Corinthians 14:11). None of this range points toward anything sudden or destructive; all of it points toward capability — the power to actually accomplish something.

That range makes Romans 1:16 land differently than the "dynamite" reading suggests: Paul isn't describing the gospel as something that detonates. He's saying the gospel carries the actual, sufficient capability to do what it claims — to save. It's a claim about effectiveness and ability, not about explosive drama.

One more data point worth checking, per step 4 above: the Reina-Valera Antigua renders this same word as potencia rather than the more common poder — a translator's choice that leans toward "capability" over generic "strength," landing on the same ability-focused sense the NT usage range points to.
