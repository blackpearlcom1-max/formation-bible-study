Faith and Works: are they really in tension?

James writes that faith without deeds is dead. Paul writes that a man is justified by faith apart from works of the law. Read as two isolated verses, that looks like a flat contradiction. The tension, what both writers actually agree on, the sourced evidence, and the question Formation doesn't answer for you.

schedule 9 min read · Topical Study · BSB Translation

The tension, stated plainly

"As you can see, a man is justified by his deeds and not by faith alone." — James 2:24, BSB
"For we maintain that a man is justified by faith apart from works of the law." — Romans 3:28, BSB

Set next to each other with no context, these two verses look like they can't both be true. James adds pressure to his side of the sentence rather than softening it: "faith by itself, if it does not result in action, is dead" (2:17), and later, "as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without deeds is dead" (2:26). Paul, writing to a different congregation entirely, states his case with just as little hedging a few verses earlier: "no one will be justified in His sight by works of the law" (Romans 3:20). Both writers are answering the same question — what makes a person right with God — and on the surface, they're naming opposite ingredients.

What both writers actually agree on

Neither writer is arguing what the isolated verse alone might suggest. James never says works apart from faith save anyone — his actual target in this chapter is a "faith" reduced to a claim with nothing behind it: "You believe that God is one. Good for you! Even the demons believe that—and shudder" (2:19). Correct belief by itself, James says, isn't even a distinctly Christian achievement; demons already have it, and it changes nothing in them. Paul, for his part, never argues that a justified life leaves no visible trace. In the same letter where he insists salvation is "not by works, so that no one can boast," he adds, one verse later, that believers are "God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance as our way of life" (Ephesians 2:8–10). Grace excludes boasting. It doesn't exclude a changed life.

The two writers even reach for the same proof text. James's argument for Abraham ends by quoting Genesis directly: "the Scripture was fulfilled that says, 'Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness'" (James 2:23, quoting Genesis 15:6) — and Paul, arguing toward a different conclusion, builds an entire chapter of Romans on that same verse. Both writers treat Abraham as the model case. They disagree about exactly what his story proves.

Two different opponents, two different questions

Paul's target in Romans and Galatians is a specific claim: that Gentile converts needed to take on "works of the law" — chiefly circumcision and the Mosaic food code — to be right with God. Galatians 2 records Paul confronting Peter over exactly this, after Peter stopped eating with Gentile believers under pressure from "the circumcision group" (Galatians 2:12). Paul's answer, stated three times in a single verse for emphasis, is that "a man is not justified by works of the law, but by faith in Jesus Christ... because by works of the law no one will be justified" (Galatians 2:16). His opponents wanted to add a requirement. Paul's whole argument is that nothing gets added.

James is answering a different question aimed at a different failure. He isn't writing against people demanding circumcision — he's writing against people already inside the church who treated "faith" as pure mental assent, disconnected from anything that follows it. His own illustration names the target directly: someone who tells a person without food or clothing "go in peace, stay warm and well fed" and provides nothing (2:15–16) has just demonstrated exactly the kind of "faith" James is attacking — the kind that changes no one's circumstances, his own included.

The same Greek word, doing two different jobs

Both writers use the Greek verb behind "justified" — dikaioō, to declare or show to be righteous — which is part of why the two verses read as a head-on collision in English. But the word doesn't have to describe only one kind of moment. Paul uses it for an initial verdict: God's declaration that a sinner is righteous, received the moment a person trusts Christ, prior to and apart from any law-keeping. James uses it for a public demonstration: Abraham was already declared righteous by faith in Genesis 15:6 — decades before he offered Isaac in Genesis 22, the event James actually points to in verse 21. James's own sentence makes the order explicit: "his faith was working with his actions, and his faith was perfected by what he did" (2:22). The faith came first. Offering Isaac showed it was real. James isn't describing how a person becomes righteous before God — he's describing how a person's faith becomes visible to everyone else, including, in his own phrase, to the "foolish man" who wants "evidence" (2:20).

Two ways Christians have held this together

Faith alone, evidenced by works

The classic Reformation formulation is often summarized this way: we are justified by faith alone, but the faith that justifies is never alone. On this reading, James and Paul aren't in tension because they're answering different questions — Paul describes the root of justification, James describes its fruit. Works contribute nothing to being declared righteous; they're the necessary, expected evidence that a real, living faith is actually present. A "faith" that produces nothing, on this view, was never the kind Paul describes either — it was the demons' kind from James 2:19, correct information with no life behind it.

Faith formed by love

A second reading, with deep roots in Catholic and Orthodox tradition, starts from Paul's own phrase in Galatians 5:6, "faith working through love," and from Thomas Aquinas's later Latin formula for it: fides caritate formata, "faith formed by love." On this reading, justifying faith was never bare intellectual assent to begin with. A faith without love or action wasn't a smaller, earlier version of real faith waiting for works to complete it — it wasn't real faith at all, because love is what faith actually consists of once it's genuine. James 2 isn't adding a missing ingredient to an otherwise-complete faith. It's naming a case where the label "faith" was being used for something that never qualified.

Stated that way, the two readings share more ground than the opening verses suggest — both insist that a professed faith which changes nothing in a person's life isn't the faith either writer is actually describing. What still divides them is a real question about the internal architecture of faith itself: whether works are faith's necessary result, arriving after it, or faith's necessary substance, present within it from the start. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here.

The sourced evidence

Abraham's two moments, twenty years apart

Genesis 15:6 — "Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" — is the verse both James and Paul quote, but they're pointing at two different moments in Abraham's life. Paul's argument in Romans 4 stops there: righteousness was credited the moment Abraham believed, with no mention yet of any action on his part. James moves the story forward roughly two decades, to Genesis 22, when Abraham actually bound Isaac to the altar. James 2's own commentary on the sequence is explicit — the offering "perfected" a faith that was already present, not one still being formed. Two writers, one shared Old Testament verse, two different points in the same story chosen to make two different arguments.

What each letter was actually written into

Galatians was written into a live, first-century controversy — the same one addressed at the Jerusalem council in Acts 15 — over whether Gentile converts needed to be circumcised and keep Mosaic food law to belong fully to God's people. Galatians 2 shows Paul treating that demand as a direct threat to the gospel itself, not a minor disciplinary dispute. James, by contrast, addresses "the twelve tribes in the Dispersion" (James 1:1) — Jewish believers already scattered outside Judea, likely by the persecution described in Acts 8:1 and 11:19 — about a danger internal to a community that had never disputed circumcision or Mosaic law in the first place. Two letters, written to two different audiences, defending against two different ways an otherwise-real gospel gets emptied out.

A word both writers use for the same failure

James's Greek word for "deeds" or "works" throughout chapter 2 is erga — the same word Paul uses for "works of the law" in Romans and Galatians. The overlap in vocabulary is part of why these verses get pulled into direct conflict at a glance. But Paul's phrase is almost always the fuller "works of the law" — tied specifically to the Mosaic law code as an entry requirement — while James's usage in chapter 2 is unqualified: he means any deed at all that gives a real faith somewhere to land, starting with the plainest example available, feeding someone who is hungry.

Questions it raises

  • James measures faith against a specific, concrete test: someone without food or clothing, met with words instead of help (2:15–16). If your own faith were measured against people you've actually encountered in need this year, would the evidence hold up the way James expects it to?
  • James and Paul both point to Abraham but read his life for different proof — Paul reads backward to the moment he first believed; James reads forward to the moment that belief was tested. When you look at your own life's turning points, are you looking for evidence of what you already believed, or trying to manufacture proof for someone watching?
  • Whether works are faith's evidence or faith's substance is a real, centuries-old division between Christian traditions that agree on far more than they disagree about here. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question — but reading both cases side by side, where do you find yourself landing, and why?
