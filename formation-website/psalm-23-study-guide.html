Passage Study

Psalm 23: the LORD is my shepherd.

Six verses, almost everyone can recite part of it, and it's still worth slowing down for. The text, what a careful reading actually observes in it, the historical background behind its imagery, and the questions it leaves open.

schedule 7 min read · All levels · BSB Translation

The text

A Psalm of David. The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. He makes me lie down in green pastures; He leads me beside quiet waters. He restores my soul; He guides me in the paths of righteousness for the sake of His name. Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me. You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies. You anoint my head with oil; my cup overflows. Surely goodness and mercy will follow me all the days of my life, and I will dwell in the house of the LORD forever.

Psalm 23, BSB.

The observation

Read it once more and notice the shift partway through. For the first three verses, David describes God in the third person — "He makes me lie down," "He restores my soul," "He guides me." It's reflective, almost like a description offered to someone else. Then, right at the mention of "the valley of the shadow of death," the address changes: "for You are with me; Your rod and Your staff." From that point through the end of the psalm, David is speaking directly to God, not about Him.

That's not a stylistic accident worth skipping past. The psalm's most vulnerable line — walking through deep darkness — is exactly where the language moves from description to direct address. Notice, too, that the poem has two distinct pictures, not one extended metaphor: a shepherd caring for sheep in verses 1–4, then a host welcoming a guest to a table in verses 5–6. Both pictures are about provision and protection, but they're different images, and the second one doesn't explain the first so much as add to it.

Finally, notice what's absent. The psalm never explains why the valley is there, never promises it will be avoided, and never says danger will stop being real. What it promises is presence inside the danger, not exemption from it.

The sourced evidence

Shepherd life behind the imagery

A shepherd in this setting didn't visit the flock — he lived with it, year-round, moving it to find food and safe water. Two details in the first two verses reflect real shepherding practice rather than generic pastoral scenery. "Green pastures" weren't a permanent backdrop; the region's dry season turned most hillsides brown for much of the year, so a shepherd had to actually know where the brief, hidden patches of good grazing were. And "quiet waters" is a specific choice of water, not just any water — fast-moving streams could sweep a sheep off its feet, so a shepherd sought out still pools or slow-moving water where a flock could drink safely. Both images describe a shepherd's skill and attentiveness, not just a pleasant setting.

The "rod" and "staff" in verse 4 are two different tools with two different jobs. The rod was a solid club, used defensively against predators. The staff was a long, often hooked implement, used to guide the flock and to reach and pull back a sheep that had wandered or gotten stuck. Together they picture two different kinds of care — one that fights off threats, one that corrects and steers — rather than a single generic symbol of comfort.

A specific valley, not just a metaphorical one

"The valley of the shadow of death" translates a Hebrew phrase that can also be rendered "deep darkness" or "deep shadow," and many readers take it as a general image for danger or grief rather than one specific place. It's also geographically plausible that David had an actual place in mind: a narrow, steep gorge runs between Jerusalem and Jericho, in the same region David moved through as a shepherd and later as a fugitive from Saul, with walls tall enough to block direct sunlight for much of the day. Whether the psalm points at one specific gorge or speaks more generally, the image is doing real geographic work, not just poetic work — it's describing a place genuinely disorienting and threatening to walk through, not an abstraction.

The table, the oil, and the cup

Verse 5 shifts from shepherd to host, and the customs behind it are customs of hospitality and protection rather than a private meal. In this cultural setting, receiving someone at a spread table was a public act — it signaled that the host was extending protection over the guest, visibly, in front of whoever might wish the guest harm. That's the force of "in the presence of my enemies": the meal itself is the declaration of protection, witnessed by the threat rather than hidden from it. Anointing a guest's head with oil was a customary gesture of honor and welcome, and a cup filled to overflowing was a visible, generous sign that the host's provision wasn't rationed.

A word worth pausing on: "follow"

The verb behind "goodness and mercy will follow me" is a Hebrew word that more often means to pursue or chase — the same word used elsewhere for an enemy hunting someone down. Its use here is a real reversal of the word's normal direction: instead of being chased by a threat, the psalm pictures goodness and steadfast love actively pursuing the speaker, every day. It's a stronger, more active image than "will accompany me" or "will follow along," and it's easy to miss without checking the word.

Questions it raises

  • David wrote this while his own life included real war, betrayal, and pursuit by enemies — the danger in the psalm wasn't hypothetical for him. What does it look like to trust "You are with me" while the valley is still actually happening, rather than only in hindsight once it's over?
  • The psalm shifts from talking about God to talking to God exactly at its most vulnerable line. Is that shift something to imitate deliberately in your own prayer during hard seasons, or is it simply describing what tends to happen naturally?
  • The table is prepared "in the presence of my enemies" — not after they're gone. What would it mean to receive provision or rest in the middle of an unresolved conflict, rather than waiting for the conflict to end first?
