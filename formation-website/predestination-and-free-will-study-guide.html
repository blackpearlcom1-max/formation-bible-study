Difficult Question

Predestination and free will — are you chosen, or do you choose?

Ephesians 1 says God chose believers in Christ "before the foundation of the world" and predestined them for adoption "according to the good pleasure of His will." Read alongside that, the same Bible has Moses and Joshua command Israel, generations apart, to "choose life" and "choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve" — language that only seems to make sense if the choice is real. The tension, what both readings agree on, the sourced evidence, and the question Formation doesn't answer for you.

schedule 11 min read · Topical Study · BSB Translation

The tension, stated plainly

"For He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless in His presence. In love He predestined us for adoption as His sons through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of His will." — Ephesians 1:4-5, BSB
"But if it is unpleasing in your sight to serve the LORD, then choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve... As for me and my house, we will serve the LORD!" — Joshua 24:15, BSB

Ephesians 1 opens with about as strong a claim for divine initiative as Scripture makes: before the universe existed, before Israel existed, before any human being had done anything at all, God chose those who would belong to Him and predestined them for adoption, "according to the good pleasure of His will" — a phrase that locates the reason entirely inside God's own will. Joshua's farewell address at Shechem puts the weight somewhere else: "choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve," spoken to a room full of people who could, in that moment, actually go either way. Joshua wasn't the first to frame it this way — Moses had said nearly the same thing a generation earlier, on the plains of Moab: "I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing. Therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19). Two leaders, decades apart, both telling Israel the decision is genuinely theirs to make — while an apostle, writing to a Gentile church, tells them the decision was settled before the world began.

What both threads actually agree on

The same letter that contains the Bible's strongest predestination language does not treat human response as optional. Ephesians 1:13 moves directly from the predestination passage into "having heard and believed the word of truth... you were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit" — hearing and believing are real actions the Ephesians actually performed, not a formality layered on top of a decision already made without them. Paul makes the same move even more explicitly in Philippians: "continue to work out your salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God who works in you to will and to act on behalf of His good purpose" (Philippians 2:12-13) — one unbroken sentence that commands human effort and grounds that same effort entirely in God's prior work, without treating the two as competitors.

Paul's letter to the Romans makes the identical move at the scale of an entire argument. Romans 9 is the New Testament's most sustained defense of God's sovereign right to show mercy on whomever He chooses — "So then, it does not depend on man's desire or effort, but on God's mercy" (9:16). One chapter later, the same author writes, "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (10:13), and spends the rest of the chapter explaining why people need to hear a message preached in order to call on that name. Paul did not treat chapter 9 as canceling chapter 10, or chapter 10 as canceling chapter 9 — he wrote both, back to back, in the same letter.

Peter's second letter adds a third data point both sides treat as significant: God's own stated posture toward final judgment is patience, "not wanting anyone to perish, but everyone to come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). Whatever that patience ultimately accomplishes, the text describes it as aimed at "everyone," using the language of genuine divine desire rather than mere procedural delay.

Paul commands both in a single sentence

Philippians 2:12-13 is cited by both sides precisely because it refuses to pick one: "work out your own salvation" is second-person and active, addressed to the Philippians themselves; "it is God who works in you" is the reason given for why that command is even possible in the first place. Neither clause hedges the other — Paul places them in the same breath, without apparent tension.

Election language sits beside a call to "everyone"

Romans 9's election language and Romans 10:13's "everyone who calls" both belong to Paul, within the same letter, two chapters apart. Whatever resolves the apparent tension between them, it isn't that Paul contradicted himself without noticing — the structure of his own argument shows he intended both statements to stand together.

Two ways Christians have held this together

The unconditional election reading

On this reading, salvation begins and ends with God's initiative. Romans 9:15-16 is read at face value: mercy "does not depend on man's desire or effort, but on God's mercy" — a direct statement, not merely a description of Israel's national history. Romans 9:20-21's potter-and-clay image is read the same way Paul appears to intend it: the clay has no standing to question the potter's choices. Jesus' statement in John 6:44 — "No one can come to Me unless the Father who sent Me draws him" — is read as describing every person's actual spiritual condition apart from God's initiative, not a special exception. On this view, "choose life" and "choose whom you will serve" are real commands that expose human responsibility for rejecting God, without implying that fallen human beings possess, on their own, the spiritual ability to choose Him unaided.

A second reading holds that God's choice is real but responsive: Ephesians 1's "predestined" is read alongside Romans 8:29's "those He foreknew He also predestined," with God's foreknowledge of who would freely respond in faith as the basis for predestining, rather than an arbitrary, unconditioned decree. On this reading, Deuteronomy 30:19 and Joshua 24:15 mean what they appear to say — the choice is genuinely available to the hearer in the moment it's given. John 6:44's "drawing" is read as something offered widely rather than selectively, consistent with 2 Peter 3:9's "not wanting anyone to perish." Human freedom to respond, on this view, is itself a gift of grace rather than an unaided human achievement, but it remains a real choice with a real alternative.

Both readings agree that salvation is by grace and not human merit, agree that Scripture commands genuine belief and repentance, and agree that God's mercy — not human deserving — is the reason anyone is saved at all. What still divides them is whether the "choosing" language describes a real, live possibility in the moment it's spoken, or a category that only becomes operative once God has already acted first. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here.

The sourced evidence

The potter and the clay in Romans 9

Romans 9:15's "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy" quotes God's own words to Moses in Exodus 33:19, spoken in the aftermath of the golden calf — the original context is God graciously renewing His covenant with a nation that had just broken it, not an abstract statement about individual salvation in isolation. Romans 9:17's citation of Exodus 9:16 ("I raised you up for this very purpose") applies the same sovereignty language to Pharaoh, a hostile foreign king, showing Paul willing to extend the principle even to someone actively opposing God's people.

"Before the foundation of the world" in Ephesians 1

Ephesians 1:11 repeats the predestination language a second time within the same chapter — believers "having been predestined according to the plan of Him who works out everything by the counsel of His will" — making it one of the most concentrated statements of divine sovereignty over salvation anywhere in Paul's letters, appearing twice within eleven verses.

"Choose this day" repeated across two covenant renewals

Deuteronomy 30:19 and Joshua 24:15 are not the same event. Moses speaks on the plains of Moab before Israel has ever entered the land; Joshua speaks decades later at Shechem, after the conquest is already complete. That two of Israel's most formal covenant-renewal ceremonies, a generation apart, both use "choose" language as their climactic appeal is the strongest textual basis for reading the choice as genuinely live rather than merely rhetorical.

Questions it raises

  • Philippians 2:12-13 commands believers to "work out" their salvation and grounds that same command in God working in them "to will and to act." Does that resolve the tension between divine sovereignty and human responsibility, or does it just restate both sides in one sentence without explaining how they fit together?
  • Paul writes Romans 9's election language and Romans 10:13's "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" within two chapters of the same letter. What does it tell you that the same author held both without apparently seeing them as contradictory?
  • Whether God's predestining choice is unconditional or based on foreknowledge of a real human response is a real, long-held difference among sincere, Bible-believing Christians — the Reformed and Arminian traditions have debated it for centuries. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here — but reading Ephesians 1, Deuteronomy 30:19, Joshua 24:15, and Romans 9 side by side, where do you find yourself landing, and why?
