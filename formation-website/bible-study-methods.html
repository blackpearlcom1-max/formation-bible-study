Five methods, compared

There's no single "correct" way to study — the right method depends on how much time you have and what you're trying to get out of a session. Here's how five well-known approaches, all guided on this site, actually differ.

Inductive Study (Observe, Interpret, Apply)

The foundation method Formation is built around. Slow down before a passage, notice what's actually on the page, ask what it meant to its first readers, then ask what it means for you.

Best for: unhurried, close study of a passage or a full book — when you want to go deep, not just read for the day.

SOAP Method (Scripture, Observation, Application, Prayer)

A short, four-step daily habit: write out a verse, note one observation, name one application, close in a short prayer.

Best for: a repeatable morning or evening routine when you have ten or fifteen minutes, not an hour.

Topical Study

Trace one theme — hope, the Kingdom of God, forgiveness — across multiple books, without cherry-picking verses out of context.

Best for: answering a specific question ("what does the whole Bible say about …") rather than working through one passage or book.

Word Studies

Go deep on a single significant word — its range of meaning in the original Hebrew or Greek, and where else the same word shows up — without needing to read either language yourself. Pairs with Greek Interlinear for Beginners.

Best for: the moment a translation choice ("no condemnation," "I shall not want") makes you want to know what's really being said.

Book-Survey Method

Before studying any single passage, get oriented in the whole book — who wrote it, who it was written to, and the argument or narrative arc that holds every chapter together.

Best for: starting a new book, so every passage study afterward has context instead of floating alone.

The same verse, four different ways

To make the comparison concrete, here's how four of the methods above would each open on the same verse, Psalm 23:1 — "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

Inductive

Observe first: what single word describes the psalmist's condition, and what does that word actually mean? The Hebrew behind "want" is chaser — "to lack," not "to desire." That single observation reframes the whole verse before you interpret or apply anything.

SOAP

Write out the verse. Observation: "want" here means lack, not desire. Application: name one thing you've been treating as a need that's actually a want. Prayer: thank God for provision, honestly, in under two minutes.

Topical

Pull the thread on "provision" across Scripture — Philippians 4:19 ("my God will supply all your needs"), Matthew 6:33 ("all these things will be added to you"), Ezekiel 34:15 ("I will tend My flock"). Psalm 23:1 becomes one data point in a larger biblical pattern, not an isolated promise.

Word Study

Trace chaser elsewhere — Deuteronomy 2:7 ("you have not lacked anything"), Nehemiah 9:21 ("they lacked nothing" in the wilderness). The same root shows the psalm is making a claim about God's track record with an entire nation, not just one shepherd-poet.

Common questions

Do I have to pick one method and stick with it?

No. Most regular readers rotate — SOAP on a rushed weekday morning, inductive study on a slower Saturday, a topical study when a specific question comes up in conversation. All of them work inside Formation.

Which method should a complete beginner start with?

SOAP or the book-survey method are usually the easiest on-ramps — SOAP because each session is short and repeatable, book-survey because it gives you orientation before you're expected to interpret anything. How to Study Without Seminary covers this in more depth.

Do I need to know Greek or Hebrew to do a word study?

No. Greek Interlinear for Beginners and Word Studies 101 both walk through how to use original-language tools without reading either language yourself.

How does color-coding fit into all this?

Color-coding isn't a separate method so much as a marking system you can layer onto any of the five above — a simple way to track themes, repetition, and structure as you read, whichever method you're using that day.
