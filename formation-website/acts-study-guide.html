Book Guide

Understand Acts.

Luke's sequel to his Gospel doesn't hide its outline — Jesus states it Himself in the book's eighth verse, and the next twenty-seven chapters simply follow it. Acts is the hinge of the New Testament: it's the only book that shows how a handful of frightened disciples in a locked room became a movement that reached the capital of the empire, and it does it as narrative, not doctrine.

schedule 12 min read · Beginner · Berean Standard Bible

Who wrote it, and why it exists

Acts is the second half of a two-volume work. The same author who wrote the Gospel of Luke — addressed to "Theophilus" in both openings — picks the story back up exactly where the Gospel left off, at Jesus' ascension, and carries it forward roughly thirty years. Early church tradition and the book's own internal evidence both point to Luke, a physician and traveling companion of Paul (Colossians 4:14): several passages abruptly shift from "they" to "we" (Acts 16:10 and following), which reads as the natural mark of someone who was actually present for those legs of the journey rather than reporting secondhand.

The book most likely closes around AD 62, because it ends with Paul still under house arrest in Rome rather than describing his trial's outcome, release, or death — the kind of detail a later writer summarizing his life would be unlikely to leave out. Whatever "Theophilus" means for the original audience — a single named patron or a broader term for "one who loves God" — Luke's stated purpose in his first volume was to give an orderly account so the reader could "know the certainty" of what they'd been taught. Acts continues that project past Jesus' earthly ministry and into the life of the church He left behind.

The structure: one verse gives you the whole outline

Most books of the Bible require some work to find their outline. Acts states its own directly. In 1:8, the risen Jesus tells the apostles: "you will be My witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth" — and that's not just a promise, it's a table of contents. The rest of the book moves through exactly that geography, in that order.

Ch. 1–7Jerusalem

The Spirit falls at Pentecost, Peter preaches the first sermon of the church age, and thousands are added in a single day. The young church grows fast enough to need its first structural fix — appointing seven men to oversee food distribution — before Stephen, one of those seven, becomes its first martyr. His death triggers the scattering that opens the next phase.

Ch. 8–12Judea, Samaria, and the Door to the Gentiles

Scattered believers carry the message outward. Philip preaches in Samaria and explains Isaiah to an Ethiopian official on a desert road. Saul — the church's most effective persecutor — is confronted by the risen Jesus on the road to Damascus and becomes its most effective missionary. The turning point: Peter is sent to a Roman centurion named Cornelius, and the Spirit falls on Gentiles before baptism or circumcision even enter the conversation.

Ch. 13–19To the Ends of the Earth

Paul and Barnabas are sent out from Antioch, and the story's geography widens across Cyprus, Asia Minor, and Greece. Midway through, a real and sharp disagreement over John Mark splits Paul and Barnabas into two teams instead of one — the mission keeps moving even through genuine conflict between its own leaders. Paul reasons in synagogues, marketplaces, and eventually a philosophers' council in Athens, meeting Jew and Gentile with the same core message delivered two different ways.

Ch. 20–28Arrest, Trials, and Rome

Paul is arrested in Jerusalem and spends the rest of the book in Roman custody, moved through a sequence of hearings — the Sanhedrin, governor Felix, governor Festus, King Agrippa — before appealing to Caesar as a Roman citizen. A shipwreck en route and a viper bite on Malta later, he arrives in Rome. The book ends not with his death but with him under house arrest for two years, "boldly and freely" proclaiming "the kingdom of God" and teaching "about the Lord Jesus Christ" (28:30–31).

The Jerusalem Council: the church's first real test (Acts 15)

By chapter 15, the gospel had already reached Gentiles in real numbers, and the church faced its first major internal argument: did a Gentile have to become a Jewish proselyte — circumcised, keeping the law of Moses — to be saved? This wasn't an abstract theological question. It determined whether the movement Jesus started would remain a Jewish sect or become something genuinely open to "all the nations."

Peter speaks first, appealing to his own experience with Cornelius back in chapter 10 — God had already given the Gentiles the Holy Spirit "just as He did to us," making no distinction between the two groups. Paul and Barnabas add their own testimony of what God had done among the Gentiles on their journey. Then James, leading the Jerusalem church, renders the decision, citing the prophet Amos to show that Gentile inclusion had been part of the plan all along:

"On the contrary, we believe it is through the grace of the Lord Jesus that we are saved, just as they are." — Acts 15:11 (BSB)

The council's letter to Gentile believers states the decision's basis directly: "It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us not to burden you with anything beyond these essential requirements" (15:28) — four practical restrictions, aimed at preserving fellowship and avoiding pagan associations, not at requiring law-keeping for salvation. It's a real example of a leadership body reasoning from experience, Scripture, and communal discernment together, arriving at a decision that shaped everything downstream in the book — without it, the missionary journeys of chapters 13–28 would have run into an entirely different, and far more divided, church.

Worked example: Paul at the Areopagus, observed and interpreted

"Then Paul stood up in the meeting of the Areopagus and said, 'Men of Athens, I see that in every way you are very religious. For as I walked around and examined your objects of worship, I even found an altar with this inscription: TO AN UNKNOWN GOD. Therefore what you worship as something unknown, I now proclaim to you.'" — Acts 17:22–23 (BSB)

Observation: Two chapters earlier, in the Thessalonian synagogue, Paul's method was to "reason... from the Scriptures, explaining and proving that the Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead" (17:2–3) — an argument built entirely from the Hebrew Bible, aimed at an audience that already accepted its authority. At the Areopagus, Paul quotes zero Old Testament verses. Instead he opens from something he'd actually observed walking through Athens — a specific altar inscription — and later cites two Greek poets, not Moses or the prophets, to make his point about humanity's relationship to God.

Interpretation: This isn't Paul softening or hiding his message — the speech still ends exactly where his synagogue sermons end, at the resurrection and a call to repent before coming judgment (17:30–31), and it's that very point, the resurrection, where his Athenian audience splits into mockery, curiosity, and a few genuine converts. What changes is the starting point, not the destination. Paul finds the piece of his audience's own religious life that already gestures toward the true God — their admission, via that altar, that something in their system was unknown to them — and builds from there instead of starting somewhere his listeners had no reason to trust.

Application: The method here isn't "quote Scripture" or "quote culture" as competing strategies — it's reading the room correctly and adjusting the entry point while holding the core message fixed. Paul in the synagogue and Paul on Mars Hill are making the same case to two audiences who need to hear it built from different foundations.

Study Acts in Formation

Because Acts 1:8 gives you the book's own outline, it's a natural book to track geographically — color-code by location (Jerusalem, Judea/Samaria, the missionary cities, Rome) and the book's actual shape becomes visible as you read. Ask the Word's cross-reference mode can also place Acts 15:11 directly alongside Galatians 2, Paul's own account of the same council, so you can compare both records of the same event side by side.
