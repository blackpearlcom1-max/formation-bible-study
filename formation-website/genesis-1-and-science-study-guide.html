Difficult Question

Genesis 1 and science — how do we read six days of creation?

Genesis 1 describes God forming the heavens and the earth across six days, "and there was evening, and there was morning — the sixth day." Modern cosmology and geology describe a universe and planet billions of years old. Read together, the same Bible ties its own weekly Sabbath law to a six-day pattern and declares that creation itself testifies to God's glory. The tension, what both readings agree on, the sourced evidence, and the question Formation doesn't answer for you.

schedule 10 min read · Topical Study · BSB Translation

The tension, stated plainly

"In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth... God saw all that He had made, and it was very good. And there was evening, and there was morning — the sixth day." — Genesis 1:1, 31, BSB
"The heavens declare the glory of God; the skies proclaim the work of His hands." — Psalm 19:1, BSB

Genesis 1 is not vague about its own structure. It moves through six clearly numbered days — light, sky, land and vegetation, sun and moon and stars, sea creatures and birds, land animals and mankind — each one closing with the same refrain, "and God saw that it was good," before culminating in the sixth day's "very good." Modern astronomy dates the universe's beginning to roughly 13.8 billion years ago, and modern geology dates the earth itself to roughly 4.5 billion years, both estimates drawn from independent lines of physical evidence — the cosmic microwave background, the expansion rate of galaxies, radiometric dating of rock formations. A plain reading of six ordinary days sits uneasily beside a timeline measured in billions of years. Psalm 19:1 raises the stakes rather than settling them: if the skies themselves are a form of testimony from God, then what they're read as saying matters, and Scripture doesn't tell the reader in advance how to weigh one form of testimony against the other.

What both threads actually agree on

Whatever a reader concludes about the length of the days, Genesis 1 itself is making a claim neither science nor a rival ancient creation story shares: the world is not an accident, not the leftover of a divine power struggle, and not self-existent. It is spoken into being, on purpose, by one God, in an order that builds toward something — and that something is called "very good" at the end (1:31), not merely functional or finished. The refrain "and God saw that it was good" appears after nearly every day's work, six times before the climactic seventh use in verse 31. That is a text making a claim about purpose and value, not a text primarily concerned with handing later readers a chronometer.

Exodus 20 shows that the six-day pattern was never treated by the text as decorative background. The Fourth Commandment ties Israel's entire weekly rhythm directly back to Genesis 1: "For in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth and the sea and all that is in them, but on the seventh day He rested. Therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day and set it apart as holy" (20:11). The same Hebrew word for "day" used of Israel's own six days of labor and seventh day of rest is used, in the same verse, of the six days of creation. Whatever the days of Genesis 1 are, Exodus 20:11 treats them as meaningful enough to build a nation's entire legal calendar on.

Genesis 1's own footnote-level detail also matters to both sides. The Hebrew behind Genesis 1:5's "one day" is, per the BSB translators' note, literally "day one" — an unusual ordinal construction that both plain-day and framework readers have to account for on their own terms. And the sun, moon, and stars are not created until the fourth day (1:14-19), meaning the first three days are, on any reading, days without a sun to mark them by ordinary solar means. That detail is simply in the text; what it implies is where the two readings below diverge.

A repeated refrain across six days

The "and it was good" refrain, repeated after days one through five and intensified to "very good" after day six, gives Genesis 1 a literary shape that both plain-day and old-earth readers treat as intentional rather than incidental — a deliberate structure, not a loose record. Where the two readings part ways is over how much weight that structure itself should carry against the sequence being read as strict, uniform, 24-hour days.

The Sabbath command ties back to Genesis 1 directly

Exodus 20:11's use of the same word for both God's six days and Israel's six days is the single piece of textual evidence most directly cited on each side of this discussion — some read it as proof the days must be ordinary, since Israel's work-week obviously was; others read it as proof the text is using the creation week as a theological pattern for Israel's calendar, a literary template rather than a claim about the literal duration of creation itself. The verse is the same in both readings. The conclusion drawn from it is not.

Two ways Christians have held this together

The plain-reading, six ordinary-day view

On this reading, Genesis 1's days are read the same way Exodus 20:11 appears to use them — as ordinary, sequential, roughly 24-hour days, the most natural sense of the Hebrew word yom when paired with a number and with "evening and morning" as it is throughout Genesis 1. Advocates of this reading point to Exodus 20:11 as the Bible's own commentary on Genesis 1: if the fourth commandment grounds a literal six-day work week in a literal six-day creation week, treating the creation days as long ages undercuts the very comparison the text is making. On this view, apparent scientific evidence for an ancient universe is either misread, incomplete, or will eventually be reconciled with a more recent creation as understanding improves.

The framework or old-earth reading

A second reading points to the same textual details above — the "day one" construction in 1:5, the absence of a sun until day four, and the tightly patterned, almost poetic structure of the six days themselves — as evidence Genesis 1 is written to communicate order, purpose, and God's sole authorship rather than to supply a scientific chronology. On this reading, Psalm 19:1's claim that creation itself testifies to God is read as inviting careful study of creation as a second, legitimate source of truth alongside the text, one that cannot ultimately conflict with Scripture rightly understood, even where it requires reading Genesis 1's "days" as long ages or as a literary framework rather than literal 24-hour periods. Exodus 20:11 is read here as establishing a creation-to-Sabbath pattern theologically, not as a scientific claim about duration.

Both readings agree Genesis 1 is deliberately structured, agree it is making a real historical claim that God alone created everything that exists, and agree it is not myth in the sense of being unconcerned with truth. What still divides them is whether the text's plain grammatical sense or its literary signals should carry the final word on the length of the days. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here.

The sourced evidence

Genesis 1's own literary structure

The BSB's translators' note on Genesis 1:5 flags that the Hebrew reads literally "day one," an ordinal construction distinct from the "second day," "third day" pattern used for the rest of the chapter — a textual detail every reading has to account for on its own terms, whether as an idiom with no special weight or as a signal the text is doing something more literary than a running calendar entry. Genesis 1:14-19 places the sun, moon, and stars on the fourth day, after light and the day-night division already exist on day one — a sequence both readings must explain, since ordinary solar days are difficult to define before the sun exists.

Exodus 20:11 anchors the Sabbath in a six-plus-one pattern

Exodus 20:11 is the Old Testament's own cross-reference back to Genesis 1, written centuries later as part of the Ten Commandments given at Sinai. It uses the same Hebrew word for "day" to describe both the days of creation and the days of Israel's work week within a single sentence, making it the most direct textual link between the creation account and ordinary human time-keeping anywhere in Scripture — and the primary verse both readings return to when arguing their case.

Psalm 19 as a Biblical category for general revelation

Psalm 19:1's claim that "the heavens declare the glory of God" is not an isolated poetic flourish — the psalm goes on to describe the sun's daily circuit across the sky in verses 4-6 before turning, in verse 7, to "the law of the LORD," pairing what creation itself communicates with what the written law communicates in the same composition. This is the textual basis old-earth readings point to for treating careful observation of the physical universe as a legitimate, God-given source of truth worth taking seriously alongside the text, rather than as a rival to it.

Questions it raises

  • Exodus 20:11 uses the same word for God's six creation days and Israel's six work days in a single verse. Does that connection settle the question of what kind of "day" Genesis 1 means, or does it show the text using the creation week as a pattern without settling the question either way?
  • Genesis 1:5's "day one" wording and the sun's late arrival on day four are details built into the text itself, not objections invented by modern science. Does knowing these details are already there change how you read the rest of the chapter?
  • Whether Genesis 1's days are best read as ordinary 24-hour days or as a literary framework for long ages is a real, long-held difference among sincere, Bible-believing Christians. Formation doesn't adjudicate that question here — but reading Genesis 1, Exodus 20:11, and Psalm 19 side by side, where do you find yourself landing, and why?
